Euler Number Tutorial

The Euler number applies wherever a fluid has to be pushed through something that resists it, such as a filter, a valve, a bed of packed pebbles or a long pipe. Two things compete. The pressure force pushes the fluid through. Inertia is the force the moving fluid carries with it. Eu compares them, and so it tells us how hard the fluid has to be pushed, compared with how fast it is going. Every animation below can be changed with the sliders.

Step 1 of 4

Pressure force and inertia in a filter

To get water through a filter or a bed of grains, the pressure at the inlet must be higher than at the outlet. Acting on a fluid element of size L, this pressure difference Δp gives a net push of Fp ~ Δp L² (pressure times area). The moving fluid also carries inertia, Fi ~ ρU²L², the force scale from the previous chapter. The two gauge tubes show the pressure on each side of the grains, and the two arrows on the right show both force scales for one fluid element. The arrow lengths follow a logarithmic scale, so that a factor of ten always looks the same. The fluid is water, ρ = 1000 kg/m³.

Step 2 of 4

The ratio is the Euler number

Dividing the pressure force scale by the inertial force scale, the area L² cancels:

Eu = Fp / Fi = Δp L² / ρU²L² = Δp / ρU²

The balance tips to the left when the pressure force is larger (Eu above 1). That means that the fluid has to be pushed much harder than its own motion would suggest, as in a fine filter. It tips to the right when inertia is larger (Eu below 1), as in a wide-open pipe. The tilt follows log10(Eu).

Step 3 of 4

Eu belongs to the obstruction

Every fitting, filter or bed has its own Eu, which is set mainly by its shape. The plot below shows the pressure along the pipe divided by ρU². The pressure falls across the obstruction by exactly Δp / ρU² = Eu. Because the plot is divided by ρU², it does not change when you change the speed, even though the real Δp in the readout does. The narrowing of the gap makes the fluid speed up through it, which is why a larger Eu needs more pressure. Many engineers quote the loss coefficient K = Δp / (½ρU²) instead, which is simply K = 2 Eu.

The values on the buttons are typical orders of magnitude. The real values depend on the exact design.

Step 4 of 4

Why speed is so expensive

For a given obstruction at high speed, Eu stays the same, so Δp = Eu ρU². Doubling the speed quadruples the pressure drop. The pumping power per unit of pipe area is Δp × U, so it grows as U³, and doubling the speed needs eight times the power. Halving the speed saves seven eighths of it. The sliders set Eu and the speed U compared with a reference speed U₀ = 1 m/s of water.

This holds for turbulent flow and for sharp-edged fittings, where Eu is nearly constant. In slow, smooth (laminar) flow through fine filters and packed beds, viscosity dominates. There Eu itself falls as the speed rises (Eu ∝ 1/Re), and Δp grows only in proportion to U.

Rules of thumb

EuWhat it meansTypical examples
≲ 0.1The pressure loss is small compared with the inertia of the flow. The fluid passes through easily.Short straight pipes, wide-open fittings.
~ 1The pressure force and inertia are comparable.Orifice plates, valves, bends and tees.
≫ 10The pressure force dominates. The fluid has to be pushed hard to get through, and the pumping cost is high.Fine filters, membranes, packed beds. Long pipes also, since Eu = fL / 2D.

Some books define Eu with ½ρU² in the denominator, and then call it the loss coefficient K or the pressure coefficient Cp. Check which definition is used before comparing values: K = 2 Eu. Euler scaling: a small model of a valve, filter or pipe network gives the same pressure drop in units of ρU² as the full-size one when the geometry is similar and the Reynolds numbers match.
