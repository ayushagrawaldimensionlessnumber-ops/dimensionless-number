Reynolds Number Tutorial

The Reynolds number tells you which force wins in a flowing fluid. Two forces compete. Viscous force drags layers of fluid together and smooths the flow. Inertial force keeps fluid moving and lets disturbances grow. Re is their ratio. Each animation below can be changed with the sliders.

Step 1 of 4

Viscous force: layers drag on each other

A moving plate drags the layer of fluid touching it. That layer drags the next one, and so on down to the fixed plate. Each layer pulls its slower neighbour forward and is held back by it. The shear stress is τ = μ du/dy, and over a patch of area A the viscous force scales as Fv ~ τA ~ μUL.

The blue line is a row of tracers that starts out straight and is stretched by the shear.

Gap L = 10 mm. Water is about 10-3 Pa·s, light oil about 0.1 Pa·s, honey about 10 Pa·s.

Step 2 of 4

Inertial force: fluid resists being accelerated

When a channel narrows, the same flow must squeeze through a smaller area, so the fluid speeds up. Speeding up needs a force, and the fluid pushes back with its own inertia. Its size scales as Fi ~ ρU²L², which is density times acceleration (U²/L) times volume (L³).

The orange square is a fluid element. Its arrow gets longer where the channel is narrow.

Step 3 of 4

The ratio is the Reynolds number

Divide the inertial scale by the viscous scale and most of the terms cancel:

Re = Fi / Fv = ρU²L² / μUL = ρUL / μ

The balance below tips to the right when inertia wins (Re above 1) and to the left when viscosity wins (Re below 1). Because forces span many orders of magnitude, the tilt follows log10(Re).

Step 4 of 4

What Re looks like in a pipe

Dye is injected at five heights at the pipe inlet. At low Re viscosity keeps the layers in order and the dye stays in thin streaks. As Re rises, inertia lets small disturbances grow into eddies that mix the dye across the whole pipe. Here L is the pipe diameter.

Rules of thumb

ReWhat dominatesWhat you see
< 1Viscous forceCreeping flow: swimming microorganisms, honey, polymer melts
1 to 2,000Viscous force, stillLaminar flow: ordered layers, parabolic profile in a pipe
2,000 to 4,000Neither clearlyTransition: bursts of turbulence come and go
> 4,000Inertial forceTurbulent flow: eddies, strong mixing, higher friction

The limits 2,000 and 4,000 are the usual values for flow in a round pipe with L equal to the diameter. Other geometries, such as flow past a sphere or over a flat plate, have different limits.
